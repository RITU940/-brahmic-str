\section{What Predicts Transfer?}
\label{sec:analysis}

This section reports the campaign's confirmatory analysis exactly as preregistered, including the
falsification of our own primary hypothesis.

\subsection{The preregistered primary fails}
\label{sec:fertility-fails}

Fertility --- the property that governs the supervised grapheme-vs-BPE trade-off
(Sec.~\ref{sec:pivot}) --- was preregistered as the primary transfer predictor (H3). It is
refuted: Spearman $\rho=\spearFert$ across the nine Rung-B results --- directionally
\emph{opposite} to the hypothesis. The flagship case was called in advance by the declared
competitor: our prospective prediction file ranked Malayalam best under fertility (P1) and worst
under coverage (P2); it realized bottom-tier (\wrrBmalayalam\ WRR). We report this as a genuine
falsification, consistent with fertility skepticism in the LLM-tokenizer
literature~\cite{beyondfertility} and with the data-centric view of STR
transfer~\cite{crosslingstr}.

\paragraph{What fertility does predict (exploratory).} A post-hoc analysis --- not
preregistered, and labeled accordingly --- shows that while fertility fails to predict
\emph{who transfers}, it predicts \emph{who needs the pivot}: across the nine scripts, the
BPE-ablation penalty ratio $\mathrm{B}/\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{bpe}}$ correlates with fertility at
Spearman $\rho=\spearFertPenalty$ (exact permutation $p=\spearFertPenaltyP$). The
lowest-fertility script (Devanagari) is precisely where BPE comes closest to parity
($\bpeRatioMin\times$), and the highest-fertility scripts (Tamil, Malayalam) are where it
collapses ($\bpeRatioMax\times$, $2.31\times$). This mirrors, in the zero-real-image regime,
the supervised relationship between fertility and grapheme advantage that motivated the pivot
(Sec.~\ref{sec:method}). Two cautions: the ratio shares a denominator with absolute transfer
(high-fertility scripts also transfer worst, so small denominators inflate ratios --- on the
absolute difference $\mathrm{B}-\mathrm{B}_{\mathrm{bpe}}$ the correlation drops to
$\rho=\spearFertPenaltyDiff$), and with $n=9$ this is a candidate regularity for confirmatory
testing on new scripts, not a law.

\subsection{The two-factor account}
\label{sec:twofactor}

Two result-blind quantities explain the campaign. \textbf{(i) Synthetic budget dominates.} The
two scripts with the disclosed $2\times$ synthetic budget (Bengali, Devanagari) are the top two
results (\wrrBbengali, \wrrBdevanagari\ WRR) --- Bengali from the second-\emph{lowest} coverage.
\textbf{(ii) Coverage orders transfer within a budget.} Among the seven equal-budget scripts,
pivot-space token coverage correlates at $\rho=\spearCovEqual$ (raw, all nine: \spearCovRaw). The
joint fit
$\mathrm{WRR} = \tfIntercept + \tfCovCoef\cdot\mathrm{cov} + \tfTwoxBonus\cdot\mathbb{1}[2\times]$
attains RMSE \tfRMSE\ WRR across all nine scripts, with both $2\times$ residuals near zero. With
only nine points the coefficients are loosely determined, and we say so: a script-level bootstrap
(5000 resamples) puts the $2\times$ bonus at \ciTfTwox\ but the coverage slope at \ciTfCov\ ---
\emph{spanning zero}. We therefore read coverage as an in-sample ordering, not an established
cross-script driver, the same reading its out-of-sample failure forces
(Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling}, $r=\covOOSr$ across the six unswept scripts). A frozen-encoder
visual-similarity control --- rendered glyphs embedded by the recognizer's own vision encoder ---
predicts nothing ($\rho=\spearVissim$), separating any structural coverage effect from the
visual-similarity account of~\cite{crosslingstr}.

\subsection{Prospective scorecard}
\label{sec:scorecard}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics{figs/fig4_receipts.pdf}
  \caption{Prospective scorecard: preregistered prediction vs.\ realized WRR for every
  quantitative forecast filed before its run, scored against the rule fixed at filing time (shaded
  $\pm1$ RMSE of the instrument). The two LOSO point calls land on the diagonal; the scaling rungs
  fall off it in the two directions the text describes --- under-predicted at low budget (concave
  transfer), over-predicted at high budget (the magnitude miss). Hits and misses are both shown.}
  \label{fig:receipts}
\end{figure}

Three forecasts were filed before their runs; all are scored (Fig.~\ref{fig:receipts}).
\textbf{Kannada}: exploratory point $\predKannada$ WRR, filed with two scripts observed;
realized \realKannada. \textbf{Devanagari}: directional call (high, ${\sim}25+$) committed before
the result existed but pushed after --- we therefore label it only weakly prospective and lean on
it for nothing. \textbf{Gurmukhi} (final script): point \predGurmukhi\ WRR, bands
$\pm1\sigma$ \predGurmukhiOneSig\ / $\pm2\sigma$ \predGurmukhiTwoSig, committed \emph{and pushed}
3.7 hours before training began; realized \realGurmukhi\ --- outside the $\pm1\sigma$ band
(under-called by $1.5\sigma$), inside $\pm2\sigma$. The declared rank bet between the two
preregistered predictors resolved for coverage over fertility ($\rho$ $+0.32$ vs.\ $-0.71$ on the
seven forecast scripts), with neither ranking alone significant --- which is why the two-factor
account, not a single-predictor law, is our claim. The model's honest summary: transfer is
forecastable to roughly $\pm\tfRMSE$ WRR ($1\sigma$) from two quantities computable
\emph{before} training --- and the misses are part of the record.
