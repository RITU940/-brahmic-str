Absolute numbers need a scale, and the supervised ceiling alone is the wrong one. Against the
\textbf{ceiling} we recover \oursOverCeiling\ of the macro-average (\wrrBmean\% vs.\
\parseqMean\%): real labels remain worth roughly $\ceilingOverOurs\times$ what our synthetic pivot
buys, and we do not present that as close. Against the \textbf{floor} the picture inverts. Stock
Tesseract ships a per-script model for every script here, trained on real target images, and is
what a practitioner deploys today: it reaches \tessMean\%,
and our zero-real-image model is ahead by $\tessMarginMean$ points on macro-average, losing only
Tamil. It is a document engine outside its design domain, so this is no claim to be the better
recognizer, only evidence that the zero-real-image regime has passed what is obtainable off the
shelf.

\subsection{The pivot is the mechanism: BPE ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}

Removing the grapheme-cluster vocabulary, with the same pivot strings, images and seed,
degrades all nine scripts (Table~\ref{tab:main}), from $\bpeRatioMin\times$ (Devanagari,
\wrrBdevanagari\ $\rightarrow$ \wrrPdevanagari\ WRR) to $\bpeRatioMax\times$ (Tamil,
\wrrBtamil\ $\rightarrow$ \wrrPtamil). The margin varies, the direction never does (exact
two-sided sign test, $p=\bpeSignP$). The bootstrap makes the per-script margin concrete: on
\bpeCIdisjoint/9 scripts the Rung-B 95\% CI clears the B$_{\mathrm{bpe}}$ interval outright
(Table~\ref{tab:main}); the three exceptions, Devanagari, Kannada and Gujarati, are exactly the
near-parity scripts ($\bpeRatioMin$--$\bpeRatioNearParity\times$) --- consistent with the direction holding but the
magnitude shrinking as fertility falls. The character-accuracy pattern is diagnostic: on Telugu,
Kannada and Devanagari the BPE model \emph{equals or exceeds} the pivot model's character accuracy
(Telugu \chaPtelugu\% vs.\ \chaBtelugu\%), yet word accuracy drops in every case. Synthetic fine-tuning alone teaches the model to see the characters. The
cluster-level output space is what assembles them into correct whole words.

\subsection{Dose--response: synthetic budget}
\label{sec:scaling}

We hold the recipe fixed and vary only the number of synthetic target images, for the three
scripts whose held-out budget we can grow (Malayalam, Kannada, Telugu) across
$\{810, 1620, 6480, 12960\}$ renderings; the external Rung-B point (3240) anchors each curve.
Word recognition is cleanly log-linear in synthetic exposure ($R^2$ from \scaleRtwoMin\ to
\scaleRtwoMax\ per script), and the slope is \emph{script-invariant}: a single shared
$\scaleSlope$ WRR per doubling fits all three, with no evidence of differing slopes ($F$-test
$p=\scaleFp$; Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}). This is the preregistered \emph{form}, and it holds.

The preregistered \emph{magnitude} does not. The frozen instrument projected $+\tfTwoxBonus$ WRR
per doubling, transported from the in-script $2\times$-synthetic experiment that fit the two-factor
model (Sec.~\ref{sec:analysis}). The swept slope excludes that by a factor of $\scaleSlopeRatio$,
with the faithful doubling step ($3240\!\rightarrow\!6480$) gaining $\scaleFaithfulObs$ WRR against
a predicted $\scaleFaithfulPred$, outside the $\pm2$\,RMSE band and reported as filed (open
squares, Fig.~\ref{fig:scaling}). Two further preregistered edges resolve the same way: the
low-budget prediction of near-collapse toward the Rung-A floor is \emph{falsified} (Kannada and
Telugu already reach double digits at 810 images, so transfer is concave in log-budget), and the
top step ($6480\!\rightarrow\!12960$), which adds renderings at near-fixed lexicon, gains little on
Kannada but keeps pace on Malayalam and Telugu. Synthetic scaling is real and orderly, then, but
far shallower than an in-script extrapolation predicts. We do \emph{not} promote pivot-space
coverage to a cross-script driver: it orders the three swept scripts in-sample, but across the six
never used in the fit it is uncorrelated with transfer ($r=\covOOSr$).

\subsection{Architecture generality: a preregistered CRNN replication}
\label{sec:crnn}

Every result above uses one backbone, inviting the objection that the effect is specific to a
pretrained vision--language model. We preregistered a replication (Amendment~6, committed before
any CRNN trained) on a \emph{from-scratch} CRNN (ResNet+BiLSTM, CTC, no large-scale pretraining)
over the same LOSO splits, pivot space and metric, for the three equal-synthetic scripts
spanning the observed Rung-B range (Tamil, Telugu, Oriya), vocabulary from TRAIN records only and a
60-epoch budget fixed in advance. Four predictions were filed; all four are scored.

The two \emph{structural} predictions hold: source-only Rung~A stays at the floor on all three
(WRR $\le\crnnAmax$, predicted $<2.0$), and the held-out ranking is preserved
($\crnnBtamil<\crnnBtelugu<\crnnBoriya$, matching the Florence-2 order), with Rung~B above Rung~A
everywhere. The two \emph{effect-size} predictions miss. The preregistered primary, a
$\ge\!+2.0$ WRR lift on at least two of three, holds only on Oriya ($+\crnnLiftOriya$). The CRNN
reaches a $\crnnRatioMean\times$ fraction of the Florence-2 transfer level on average (predicted
band $[0.15,0.60]$), inside the band on Oriya alone, and character accuracy is depressed in step
(Tamil \crnnChaBtamil\% vs.\ \chaBtamil\%): the small model sees fewer characters, not merely fewer
whole words. Per the preregistration's declared caveat we call this \emph{ambiguous} rather than
resolve it in our favour: an attenuated directional replication fits both a backbone-dependent
mechanism and a plain capacity floor, and the character accuracy supports the latter. The
direction replicates across architectures; the magnitude stays open.

\subsection{Out-of-benchmark: Khmer}
\label{sec:khmer}

Every script above shares the ISCII-aligned Brahmic design the pivot was built around, so to find
where the method stops we ran it somewhere it should struggle: Khmer, a disjoint Unicode block with
coeng-based stacking, outside the benchmark and outside India. Reaching it needed a hand-curated
pivot extension (\khmerMapRate\% of test codepoints map; the rest are approximated or dropped and
counted as misses). Under Amendment~4b, Rung~A trains on the nine benchmark scripts with no Khmer
of any kind and Rung~B adds \khmerSynth\ synthetic Khmer words, still with no real Khmer image;
both are evaluated on \khmerN\ KhmerST~\cite{khmerst} word crops. Khmer recognition is an active
area, but its systems train on real Khmer~\cite{univkhmer}, exactly the data this rung withholds. Before any Khmer
recognizer of ours existed we filed a point prediction from the frozen two-factor model,
\khmerPred\ WRR at $\pm1\cdot$RMSE \khmerBandOne, with a written caveat that we expected it to be
fragile.

It was. Rung~B reaches \khmerB\% WRR against a predicted \khmerPred: a residual of \khmerResid,
or $\khmerResidRMSE\cdot$RMSE, below even the $\pm2\cdot$RMSE band \khmerBandTwo. The miss is not
an artifact of the coverage recheck disclosed at build time: re-evaluating the same instrument
at the realized token coverage (\khmerTokCov\% rather than the filed \khmerTokCovPrereg\%) moves
the point only to \khmerPredRecheck. Only the structural prediction survives: Rung~A sits at
\khmerA\ (predicted $<5$). The instrument had already lost its slope magnitude in block
(Sec.~\ref{sec:scaling}); this is the first script we asked it to predict outside the blocks it
was fit on, and it failed there too. We do not carry it forward as a contribution.

Two things nonetheless hold. The \emph{direction} replicates ($\khmerLift$ WRR, $\khmerChaLift$
character accuracy over the no-target-data control), so the mechanism is not inert out of block;
the \emph{magnitude} collapses to \khmerPctOfMeanB\% of the nine-script Rung-B mean. For
calibration, stock Tesseract's \emph{Khmer-specific} model, trained on real Khmer and scored on
the identical crops, reaches only \tessKhmer\% here against \tessMean\% across the nine: these
crops are hard for a supervised system too. That is context, not a rescue. Both numbers sit
near the floor, and \khmerB\% is a failure to transfer usefully.

We draw the boundary claim narrowly. Khmer received the same $1\times$ synthetic budget and
comparable nominal token coverage (\khmerTokCov\%) that yield double-digit WRR in block, and still
returned \khmerB. Volume and coverage were held up; typological proximity was not; the transfer
collapsed. That is the cleanest evidence we have that relatedness, not synthetic volume, carries
the effect, but it is a single script, and we state it as suggestive rather than decisive.

\subsection{Frontier-VLM reference}
\label{sec:vlm}

We prompt Qwen2.5-VL-7B~\cite{qwen25vl} zero-shot on the same nine real test sets (fixed prompt,
greedy decoding), preregistered under Amendment~4c. Under the declared primary metric, exact
word match on the raw generation, it scores $\vlmRawPrimary\%$ on all nine, because the chat
model wraps every answer in prose (``The text in the image is~\dots''). We therefore report a
\emph{disclosed, most-favorable} re-score (Table~\ref{tab:anchors}): the first quoted span from
each generation, under the identical metric. Even so the VLM reads only the two highest-resource
scripts with any competence (Bengali \vlmWrrBengali\%, Devanagari \vlmWrrDevanagari\%), sits at or
near zero on six of the remaining seven, and often returns a wholly different script: Gurmukhi
in Devanagari, Malayalam in Bengali. Our compact pivot model leads on \vlmScriptsWon\ of nine at a
fraction of the parameters. This is one open model under one prompt and the comparison is not
like-for-like, its training data being unknown; the broader audit of \cite{glotocr}, over 100+
scripts and many models, is the load-bearing evidence that scale alone does not read these scripts.
